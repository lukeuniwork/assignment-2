On the left, you can see all the major fires over the last five years. Use the slider to view all months. With this, you can see the Victorian bushfires in 2020 and the fires that swept through northern Australia.

This data was collected through NASA’s FIRM system on the MODIS Satellites, combined with the 2021 Census data to show where major fires have occurred in recent years.

MJ stands for mega joules and GJ for giga joules, meaning 10⁶ and 10⁹ joules respectively.

The map to the right illustrates the distribution of fire frequency across Australia from 2020–2024. Northern and eastern regions show the highest concentration of fires, particularly in tropical and savanna zones. In contrast, southern inland areas show fewer fires due to cooler and wetter conditions. This reflects Australia’s strong climatic gradients influencing fire occurrence.

The plot to the left shows a clear pattern of seasonal fire behaviour. Fire activity increases sharply from September through February, aligning with spring and summer when high temperatures, low humidity, and strong winds create ideal conditions for ignition. From March to August, both the number and severity of fires decline significantly.

The calendar plot above highlights intensity peaks from September to February each year. The strong clustering of high fire power coincides with warmer months when dryness and wind speeds are greatest. The 2020 period shows elevated activity consistent with the major bushfire events that year, and notable fires in the north during October 2023.

The bar chart shows that younger and middle-aged groups (0–34 years) experience the highest fire exposure. This likely reflects demographic distribution and greater representation in fire-prone regions. Older populations show lower exposure rates, possibly due to residence patterns and reduced mobility.

This chart shows that Indigenous Australians face a higher rate of fire exposure than non-Indigenous populations. The disparity reflects environmental and socioeconomic factors, including proximity to bushland and limited fire mitigation infrastructure. This highlights the need for targeted fire management strategies and community support.
